If the Alpine Fault ruptures,
what happens to Lake Hāwea?

A first quantitative look at landslide- and fault-generated waves on a 392-metre-deep glacial lake with a fast-growing township at its southern end — and at why no such map exists yet for Hāwea, Wānaka or Queenstown.

This is a research-grade scoping study, not a hazard map. It is built from a 30 m global elevation model and a reconstructed bathymetry, because no public depth survey of Lake Hāwea exists. Treat the numbers as indicative of scale, not as design values. Its point is to show the hazard is real and worth surveying properly.
The short answers

Does a hazard map already exist for Hāwea, Wānaka or Queenstown?

No — not a published inundation map, for any of the three. The hazard is named in official documents and the groundwork is laid, but the modelling is not public. Otago Regional Council has modelled tsunami for 17 coastal locations and none of the lakes; its 2025 region-wide hazard exposure analysis covers nine hazards and lake tsunami is not among them.

How high does a lake tsunami get?

Near the slide, tens of metres — lake tsunamis are generated in confined water with no ocean to spread into. Several kilometres away, at a township, the credible band is roughly 1–10 m. That is the range this model puts at Lake Hāwea township, and it matches the only comparable New Zealand study, at Lake Tekapo.

What variables do you need?

Eight for the slide, six for the basin, and the trigger. They collapse into one dimensionless number, the impulse product parameter P, which predicts the wave almost by itself.

Question 1

The map doesn't exist — here's exactly what does

Lake Hāwea is not an overlooked lake. It is named directly in the official Alpine Fault Magnitude 8 (AF8) Hazard Scenario, the planning document the South Island CDEM groups use:

“…the Hunter Valley Fault under Lake Hawea, and a tsunami there would likely impact Lake Hawea township and possibly the hydro control dam.”

That sentence is the state of the art. There is no survey, no model and no map behind it.

The Tekapo researchers explicitly named Wakatipu, Wānaka and Taupō as lakes that should get the same treatment. Hāwea wasn't on that list — AF8 put it there separately. Ten years on, neither list has been worked through.

Question 2

How big is a lake tsunami?

Bigger than most people expect, and the reason is geometry. An ocean tsunami spreads across a basin and arrives as a long, low wave. A lake tsunami is generated a few hundred metres from the shore it then hits, in water that is deep but narrow, and there is nowhere for the energy to go.

The rule of thumb GNS uses

For earthquake-triggered failures into New Zealand glacial lakes:

  • 10³–10⁴ m³ — small debris slides and rock falls → run-up 0.5–1 m within a kilometre of the slide.
  • 10⁵–10⁶ m³ — large bedrock failures → run-up ~3–25 m, still significant 8 km away.
  • >10⁶ m³ → tens of metres in the near field.

AF8 puts the practical threshold for a hazardous wave at slides above 1000 m³ — which is a very small landslide.

A 2–5 m wave arriving at a lakefront with a few minutes' warning is a life-safety emergency, not a curiosity.

Question 3

The variables — and the one number they collapse into

Three groups of inputs. The slide, the basin it lands in, and the earthquake that starts it.

The slide

Volume 𝒱, width b, thickness s, impact velocity Vs, drop height of the centre of mass Δz, slope angle α, bulk density ρs, basal friction angle δ.

The basin

Still-water depth at impact h — the single most sensitive input — plus the full bathymetry, the shoreline topography, the shore slope β, the lake level (Hāwea is dam-controlled over an 8 m range) and bed roughness n.

The trigger

Earthquake magnitude and shaking intensity, distance from the fault, slope susceptibility, any mapped active faults under the lake, and the basin's own seiche modes.

Then it all collapses

Everything about the slide reduces to Heller & Hager's impulse product parameter, and the wave follows from it:

P = F · S½ · M¼ · [cos(6α/7)]½ F = Vs / √(g h) slide Froude number S = s / h relative slide thickness M = ρs𝒱 / (ρw b h²) relative slide mass HM = (5/9) · P0.8 · h maximum wave height xM = (11/2) · P0.5 · h where it occurs TM = 9 · P0.5 · √(h/g) its period

If you take one thing from this: P is the number to compute. The implementation used here reproduces the worked Lake Askja example from the VAW-211 manual to within 1%, which is the check that says the code is right.

Question 4

The maps

Screening all 137 km of shoreline for slopes steeper than 30° with more than 400 m of relief behind them leaves four candidate source zones — about 7% of the shore. The scenarios below all place the slide in the highest-ranked one: a 3.9 km stretch of the mid-lake east shore, 38.5° mean slope, 1125 m of relief, 17 km up-lake from the township.

Afterwards

The lake keeps ringing

Once the first wave has passed, the basin oscillates as a whole — a seiche. Solving the shallow-water eigenvalue problem on the real lake outline gives the periods at which Lake Hāwea rings, and these are the periods that long-period surface waves from a distant Alpine Fault rupture can drive directly.

The hazard does not end when the first wave has passed. Water keeps sloshing at these periods for hours, and the third oscillation can catch anyone who has gone back down to the shore.

What would make this a real study

Everything below is a limitation of this model, in rough order of how much it matters.

1. There is no bathymetric survey of Lake Hāwea

The depth field here is reconstructed from a distance-to-shore transform calibrated to the published maximum (392 m) and mean (161 m) depths. A sensitivity test shows near-field wave height varies only about ±35% across a ten-fold range of assumed impact depth, so the broad picture is more robust than that sounds — but refraction and focusing along the real lakebed are not captured. A multibeam survey is the single highest-value thing anyone could commission here, and NIWA has already done exactly this for Wānaka and Tekapo.

2. The elevation model is a surface model at 30 m

Copernicus GLO-30 includes buildings and trees and cannot resolve a lakefront. Inundation extent at the township is indicative only. LINZ holds LiDAR coverage for Otago, which is the right input.

3. The landslide is parameterised, not simulated

A coupled granular-flow model — the approach used for Lake Askja — captures the generation phase far better than empirical equations can.

4. Wave generation is extrapolated past the experimental range

The slides are wide relative to the impact-zone depth, beyond the range of the laboratory experiments the equations come from, and the near field sits at the breaking limit where amplitude has to be capped.

5. These are scenarios, not probabilities

Nothing here assigns a likelihood to any particular failure. A real study needs an earthquake-induced landslide susceptibility analysis behind it.

6. Delta collapse and dam-break are not modelled

The Hunter River delta at the head of the lake is a credible subaqueous failure source, which AF8 flags explicitly. And overtopping at the Hāwea dam is reported here as a water level, not routed down the Hāwea River to Albert Town.
